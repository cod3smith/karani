# Qualification

<Lede>
The billed tier, and the one that reads your resume. It runs over what the
pre-filter let through — a couple of dozen postings out of a couple of
thousand.
</Lede>

```bash
karani qualify --limit 20
karani qualify --agent --limit 5   # tool-using; 5-10x the cost per row
```

## What comes back

<Verdict note="karani qualify">{samples.verdicts.qualified}</Verdict>

A score, a three-way verdict, strengths with evidence, gaps with
severities and mitigations, red flags, and a recommended angle. All of it
validated against a pydantic model before it reaches the database, so a
malformed response is caught rather than stored.

The recommended positioning is the part that earns its keep. It is not a
summary of the posting, it is an argument about which of your stories to
lead with — and it is what the drafting step is handed.

## Every claim carries a quote

A strength is a claim plus the line of your own resume behind it. That is
a contract, not a style:

> A claim whose citation karani cannot verify is dropped, not repaired.

The verification is mechanical. A quote that is not a verbatim span of your
records is discarded. A rewrite that loses a figure the source had falls
back to the original. A record left with no provenance is thrown away
rather than patched up ([CLAUDE.md
4.13](https://github.com/cod3smith/karani/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)).

This is not caution for its own sake. A fabricated accomplishment survives
a resume screen and then gets probed for ten minutes by someone who
interviews for a living, and you are the one in the room.

## The three verdicts

A `maybe` is not a hedge, it is a specific thing: the model found real
overlap and a real problem, and the decision is yours.

<Verdict note="karani qualify">{samples.verdicts.maybe}</Verdict>

And a `skip` says what failed rather than just scoring low:

<Verdict note="karani qualify">{samples.verdicts.skip}</Verdict>

Notice that the skip still lists a strength. The shape failed, not the
candidate, and saying so is what makes the verdict arguable — if the
posting had offered relocation, this is a different answer.

## What goes into the prompt

Four things, in two parts.

**The stable part** — the instructions, your resume, and your taste
calibration from past verdicts. Identical for every posting in a pass.

**The volatile part** — the posting.

That split exists for prompt caching, and it is load-bearing: putting
per-posting content above the breakpoint silently kills the cache and
multiplies the bill. There is a test asserting the stable prefix is
byte-identical across postings.

Memory is injected too, scoped to the company: distilled facts from past
verdicts and outcomes, rendered as a block with an explicit instruction
that they are context and never override a dealbreaker in the posting
itself. [Memory](/guides/memory/) is how they get there.

## The feedback loop

```bash
karani verdict 1482 apply
karani verdict 1544 skip
```

Each verdict becomes a few-shot `[posting → reaction]` pair in the next
qualify prompt, and a distilled fact in the memory ledger. The pipeline's
taste converges on yours with no retraining and no eval harness — the
verdicts *are* the eval.

Disagreements teach the most. A `skip` on something scored 82 is worth ten
agreements.

## Agent mode

```bash
karani qualify --agent --limit 5
```

The qualifier gets tools — web search, page fetch, GitHub org, Wikipedia —
and gathers evidence about a company before ruling. It can find out whether
a company that says nothing about pay bands has published one elsewhere,
which is exactly the gap the pre-filter cannot close.

It costs five to ten times as much per row, so it is for top candidates and
never the daily batch ([ADR
0007](/decisions/0007-agent-mode-optional-not-default/)). The loop is
bounded on iterations and tool calls, and nudged toward a final answer when
the budget runs down.

Agent mode records what it looked at, so a verdict that leaned on a web
result says so.

## Idempotence

A posting is re-qualified when, and only when, its content hash or your
resume hash has changed. So:

- Running `qualify` twice costs nothing the second time.
- Editing your resume re-judges the whole shortlist, because what it was
  judged against changed.
- Overlapping runs are safe.

```bash
karani qualify --resume path/to/other-resume.md   # judge against a variant
```

## A fit score is not portable

This is the one thing on this page that will cost you money if you ignore
it.

A fit score is only comparable **within the model and prompt version that
produced it**. Measured over 598 rows: one model's mean score was 38, with
2 rows above 80. Another's mean was 47, with 31 above 80. A third sat at
51.

The autopilot's draft floor had been tuned against the third and matched
almost nothing under the first ([ADR
0022](/decisions/0022-conversion-ledger-and-variant-promotion/)). So:

- `karani funnel` pools fit bands only when one scorer produced every
  score, and otherwise reports per-scale with a warning.
- Every qualification row stores its model and prompt version — that is
  what the foot of the plates above says.
- **Change the qualify model and re-check `[autopilot] min_fit`.**

## Failure is a verdict, not an exception

A malformed response downgrades the row to `maybe` with an explanation,
and the batch continues. A missing API key or a spent budget swaps in the
local model and says so once. Nothing about qualification is allowed to
kill a pass — a bad model day should cost you one unreviewed posting, not
the hour.

## Prompts are versioned

`PROMPT_VERSION` and `AGENT_PROMPT_VERSION` are bumped on any material
change, and every stored row carries the version it was judged under. That
is the only thing that makes "old data" and "new data" distinguishable
after the fact, which is what A/B and rollback need.
